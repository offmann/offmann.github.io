China’s Open Source Wave

Jottings • Nov 14, 2025

AI strategy

Chinese companies are now leaders in open weight models.

Alibaba’s Qwen family covers many sizes and use cases and is marketed as a one stop solution for developers and enterprises, with full open source style access to weights and tooling. You can see this directly in their GitHub repository, the Qwen 2.5 Max announcement, and an overview of the Qwen model evolution.

DeepSeek releases competitive models with weights that anyone can download, fine tune and run locally on Hugging Face. Recent versions like DeepSeek V3 and DeepSeek R1 aim at high level reasoning and are openly downloadable as weights, including DeepSeek V3, DeepSeek R1, and the Open R1 launch recap.

In other words, if I am a developer today and I want a strong free base model, it is very likely I will reach for a Chinese model.

Why I think China is doing this

Lock in developers and ecosystems

In my view, the first reason is simple. If millions of developers build on your models, you quietly set the standard.

Open weights make it easy to:

  • Embed Qwen or DeepSeek inside products
  • Reuse their tokenizers, tools and formats
  • Host them on friendly cloud and hardware stacks

Alibaba explicitly positions Qwen as an open platform for developers on top of Alibaba Cloud and documents all the variants and use cases on its official resources, including technical documentation and the Qwen 2.5 Max blog post.

So I think the game is this. Give away the model. Make money on cloud, chips and enterprise projects around it.

Turn export controls into a software play

On the hardware side, the United States is trying to slow China down by restricting the export of advanced AI chips like Nvidia’s A100 and H100, and later even the cut down A800 and H800 variants. Policy notes and reporting describe how Nvidia had to design the H20, L20 and L2 chips specifically for the Chinese market under these constraints, including a Congressional Research Service brief and analysis from ECIPE.

In that context, open models are a kind of judo move. Even if China cannot always buy the very best chips, it can:

  • Train strong models on what it has
  • Release the weights to the world
  • Let everyone run them on any hardware they want

Analysts and open source leaders have pointed out that once weights are open, companies everywhere can use them locally without sending data to a specific country or cloud. Hugging Face.

In my opinion, this turns some of the hardware pressure into a software and standards game, where China has more room to maneuver.

Control at home, openness abroad

At the same time, China has some of the strictest rules for generative AI services. Generative AI providers must register, pass security assessments and keep outputs aligned with government guidelines. This is clear in documents like the Basic Safety Requirements for Generative Artificial Intelligence Services and overviews of China’s generative AI measures from China Law Translate and White & Case.

So I see a split:

  • The base models can be open weight and global
  • The domestic services are controlled by regulation, safety tuning and monitoring

China does not lose control at home by open sourcing. It gains influence abroad.

What this means for developers and poor countries

From a developer point of view, I think the impact is huge.

If you are in Europe, Africa, Latin America or Southeast Asia and you cannot afford to pay for expensive US APIs, Chinese open models are very attractive. You can:

  • Download them once
  • Run them on your own GPUs or on a cheaper local cloud
  • Fine tune them on your language and use case

Recent coverage and community projects around DeepSeek and Qwen show how they are becoming viable drop in replacements for many closed models, especially when cost matters more than squeezing out the last few benchmark points. Highlights include DeepSeek V3, the Open R1 announcement, and Qwen’s open resources.

In my opinion, this is soft power. If most universities, startups and governments in the Global South standardize on Chinese open models, China gets long term influence over tools, formats and even research directions.

The chip supply chain and Taiwan’s role

All of this still depends on one very physical thing. Chips.

Today, advanced AI chips come mainly from Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, TSMC. TSMC manufactures Nvidia’s GPUs and dominates the most advanced process nodes that AI models need. In recent public statements and reports, Nvidia and TSMC highlight how Blackwell class GPUs are produced in TSMC fabs, including new facilities in Arizona. Nvidia’s CEO Jensen Huang literally said that without TSMC there is no Nvidia today. You can see this in Nvidia’s manufacturing update, coverage from PC Gamer, and reporting by Reuters.

At the same time, TSMC is investing in the United States and Europe, supported by large public subsidies and national security arguments around AI chip independence. Articles on US and EU onshoring show how critical this is for the AI supply chain.

So when I think about Chinese open models, I also think about this. Even if the model weights are open, the training and large scale deployment still rely on chips that come from a very small set of fabs, with Taiwan at the center and the US trying to pull more of that capacity onshore.

This is why the AI story is never just software. It is also ship routes, fabs and export rules.

What it means for the West

For the United States and its allies, I think there are two main consequences.

First, open Chinese models make export controls harder to manage in practice. Even if you limit the best chips, capable models can leak everywhere once released, which several policy analyses now describe as a proliferation risk for advanced AI capabilities.

Second, Western companies cannot assume that closed beats open forever. If free models from China are good enough for many tasks, US and European vendors will feel pressure to match that mix of quality, cost and openness.

In my opinion, the only real answer is to compete on open source too, with strong local models, clear safety work and trusted governance.

Europe and especially France

Europe is in an interesting middle position. It is not leading on chips, but it is trying to lead on regulation and on open source.

The EU AI Act sets strict rules on high risk AI and general purpose models. At the same time, groups like the Linux Foundation Europe, Open Future and others argue that open source is key for AI sovereignty in Europe. Useful explainers include the Linux Foundation Europe AI Act explainer, analysis from Open Future, a Center for Data Innovation brief, and the Wikimedia Brussels open letter.

France is a good example of this strategy. Companies like Mistral AI develop high performance open and commercial models and present them as a way for European firms to build on local technology, with European values and law in mind. See Mistral’s official site, funding coverage, and analysis of its valuation.

I think this is Europe’s play. Use open source to stay in the game, even if the chips and the largest models still come mainly from the US and Asia.

In my opinion, China is not open sourcing its AI models out of generosity. It is a smart move in a larger game that mixes developers, poor countries, export controls and the chip supply chain.

For the rest of us, this means one thing. If we care about sovereignty and long term leverage, we cannot ignore open source. We either build our own open ecosystems, or we will end up living inside someone else’s.
